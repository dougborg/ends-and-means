Central planning is a family of institutional means, not an ideology, an ownership form, or a complete description of an economy.
The United States Controlled Materials Plan offers one bounded example: federal bodies allocated three critical metals through public agencies and privately owned production chains from April 1943 through its 1945 dismantling.

## what-does-central-planning-name

The label identifies arrangements in which authorized bodies coordinate a substantial scope through plans.
Comparison still requires separate evidence about authority, scope, information, targets, revision, enforcement, ownership, and time.

## how-did-the-plan-work

The War Production Board’s Requirements Committee estimated quarterly supplies of steel, copper, and aluminum and divided them among designated claimant agencies.
Those agencies authorized production schedules and passed allotments to prime contractors, which passed appropriate quantities to subcontractors and suppliers.
Orders reaching mills therefore reflected the approved allotment chain rather than an unlimited claim on material.

## who-held-which-authority

The WPB set the material envelope, its Requirements Committee divided initial allotments, and claimant agencies translated programs into authorizations for producers.
This was centralized allocation with distributed administration, not one office making every production decision.
It also did not transfer ownership of the firms that used the allotted materials.

## how-could-the-plan-change

Supply estimates and allotments were organized by quarter, while agencies adjusted schedules as requirements and available supplies changed.
That cycle created correction points, but speed of revision does not establish that affected workers or civilians could challenge priorities or that burdens were fairly distributed.

## what-can-the-episode-show

The WPB’s own retrospective described smoother operation during 1944 while documenting continuing changes in requirements, supply, and military programs.
Landon-Lane and Rockoff dispute the inference that CMP caused the mobilization turnaround, pointing instead to time-to-build and market coordination as rival explanations.
The episode therefore shows a concrete allocation design and a historical dispute, not proof that central planning always succeeds or fails.

## where-does-the-boundary-end

CMP governed specified critical materials within a temporary wartime mobilization that also used procurement, markets, private firms, and other controls.
It cannot stand for the whole United States economy, every wartime institution, or a timeless national model.
