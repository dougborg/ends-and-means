Authoritarianism is a contested way of classifying regimes and practices that restrict political competition or undermine accountability.
It is not a synonym for authority, autocracy, dictatorship, fascism, or totalitarianism, and one coercive act does not classify an entire political order.

## Which definition is being used?

Linz's influential regime type emphasizes limited pluralism, restricted mobilization, weak guiding ideology, and leadership exercised within unclear but generally predictable limits.
Glasius instead asks whether a pattern of action sabotages accountability, allowing analysis of authoritarian practices inside more than one regime type.

## How do autocracy and dictatorship differ?

Regimes of the World uses autocracy as a measured category and distinguishes closed from electoral autocracies while marking uncertain boundaries.
Comparative dictatorship research also separates party, military, monarchic, and personalist arrangements because rulers face different organized constraints.

## Why is totalitarianism not just stronger authoritarianism?

Linz reserved totalitarianism for an ideal type with a monistic power center, guiding ideology, and extensive mobilization.
That distinction is analytically useful without implying that every historical regime fits cleanly into one box.

## What remains disputed?

Scholars use authoritarianism, autocracy, and dictatorship with overlapping but nonidentical meanings.
The safest comparison names the definition, institution, period, and evidence instead of turning a ruler's style into a timeless national label.
