Economic democracy asks who should govern economic decisions that shape people's work and material lives.
It covers several institutional traditions, and neither ownership nor formal participation alone proves that people exercise effective democratic control.

## what-question-does-it-ask

The relevant constituency may be workers, citizens, communities, or several groups at once.
Their authority may operate within a workplace or across the wider economy.

## does-it-prescribe-one-model

No single institutional design follows from the concept.
Lisa Herzog surveys direct participation, elected worker representation, cooperatives, codetermination, unions, market rules, and democratic control of finance or productive assets.
Walter Adamson advances a narrower model that balances worker, shareholder, and community representation inside firms, illustrating why the label does not identify one settled program.

## why-are-ownership-and-control-separate

Holding shares can give workers a financial claim without giving them decisive votes or authority over management.
Conversely, codetermination can give workers representation without transferring title, so ownership, voice, representation, and practical decision power must be traced separately.

## what-can-democratic-designs-fail-to-achieve

Herzog groups serious objections around property and market choice, decision costs and competence, and the risk that formal participation will reproduce domination or fail under wider economic constraints.
Evidence from partially democratic institutions therefore cannot establish the effects of every proposed model.

## how-do-the-swedish-funds-fit

Sweden's wage-earner fund program sought greater wage-earner influence through collectively financed shareholding in a continuing market economy.
The enacted funds never became controlling owners, making them a bounded test of one mechanism rather than a definition or full realization of economic democracy.
