South Carolina's 1868 constitution removed race, color, and former enslavement as bars to the vote for male citizens aged twenty-one or older, with residence and other exclusions.
The convention rejected a motion to include women.
Black South Carolinians then held governing office under those rules.
Named testimony, federal enforcement records, and the contested 1876–1877 transfer show how coercion and control constrained that practice without proving that constitutional rights or Black political activity ended on one date.

## changed-membership

Article VIII made male United States citizens aged twenty-one or older eligible to vote without race, color, or former enslavement as a bar.
Article I separately required free and open elections and equal access to elect and be elected for people who met the constitution's qualifications.
This Case follows that formal membership, its exclusions, and its use from the 1868 convention through the transfer of South Carolina's executive office on April 11, 1877.
That transfer marks the Case boundary, rather than the end of the constitutional rules or Black political activity.

## antecedent-demands

The change followed organized demands.
Delegates to the 1865 Colored People's Convention called for equal suffrage for Black men and protested taxation without representation and denial of a voice in choosing rulers.
In August 1867, an unnamed Black organizer reported addressing large meetings in eighteen or twenty districts and establishing Leagues in ten.
His letter establishes the reach he claimed for his own work; it cannot establish statewide participation rates or the views of all freedpeople.

## eligibility-and-exclusion

The elector rule treated people already resident when the constitution was adopted differently from later residents, who needed one year in the state and sixty days in the county.
It also withheld voting and officeholding during a federal constitutional disability; while a person was kept in an almshouse, asylum, or public prison; and under the text's period-specific incapacity language.
At the same time, the constitution barred disfranchisement for an offense committed while a person was enslaved.

Electors could hold elective county office, and state office generally required elector qualifications.
The first election and legislative session had a specific residence exception, while a separate article excluded a person who denied the existence of a Supreme Being from office.
The constitution prohibited property qualifications for office and directed election laws to address power, bribery, tumult, and improper conduct.
Its House rules apportioned 124 seats by inhabitants while guaranteeing each county at least one member.

The consulted scan is a later compilation containing amendments after this Case, including material from 1878 and 1882.
Those later provisions cannot be read backward as original 1868 rules.

## convention-arguments

Inside South Carolina's convention, Alonzo Ransier opposed educational and property qualifications for voting and described suffrage as a means for Black citizens to defend themselves.
William Whipper moved to remove “male” from the elector rule and argued that women had the same claim to vote as men.
The convention rejected his motion.

Three acts followed on different dates.
The convention adopted the constitution on March 17; qualified electors voted on ratification from April 14 through 16; and Congress overrode President Andrew Johnson's veto of conditional admission legislation on June 25.
The April vote was elector ratification, not congressional ratification.

## officeholding-practice

The House historian reports Black majorities in South Carolina's state House from 1868 through 1876 and in the state Senate from 1872 through 1876.
Those are different chamber periods, rather than one undifferentiated claim about a Black-majority legislature.
They demonstrate realized governing composition under the new officeholding rules, without showing how every legislator obtained or exercised authority.

## political-coercion

Elias Hill testified that he had served as a local Union League president and held political meetings at his house.
He said masked assailants attacked and threatened him on May 5, 1871, demanding that he stop a Republican newspaper, stop preaching, renounce Republicanism publicly, and leave the League.
Alberry Bonner separately testified that Klan riding made his League stop meeting because gatherings were unsafe; the selected passage does not date that underlying event.

These are named witnesses' accounts preserved through a congressional investigation.
That archive retains their testimony while also reflecting a partisan inquiry's questions, selection, and publication, so individual accounts do not establish statewide prevalence.

## federal-enforcement

The Federal Judicial Center reports that the Enforcement Act of 1870 penalized violence or intimidation used to prevent Black citizens from voting and that the 1871 Ku Klux Klan Act criminalized intimidation of qualified voters for supporting lawful federal candidates.
In October 1871, President Grant declared upcountry counties in rebellion and suspended habeas corpus there.
Federal troops detained more than six hundred suspected participants by year's end.

The FJC reports convictions or guilty pleas in nearly all prosecuted South Carolina cases and attributes a brief halt in Klan violence to the prosecutions.
Its broader conclusion is that the prosecutions did not secure durable federal protection for Black political rights.
That later assessment extends beyond the November 1872 endpoint of the enforcement Episode and should be tested separately by county and period.

## contest-and-transfer

On July 8, 1876, several hundred Red Shirts confronted a Black militia company in Hamburg and demanded its weapons.
Mark Bradley's Army history reports six executions after most of the company surrendered.
South Carolina Attorney General William Stone's contemporary report instead states that five prisoners were deliberately killed and three wounded.
Stone also identifies James Cook as killed while leaving the armory but not as a company member, and distinguishes other nonmembers among those captured.
The sources therefore support killings and a count conflict, rather than one uncontested total or a claim that every Black victim belonged to the company.
A later investigation produced indictments but no trials.

Bradley reports that by October Democrats had organized 13,000 men in sixty-eight rifle clubs, which he describes as intended to intimidate Black militia and support Wade Hampton's campaign.
Grant ordered the clubs to disband on October 17 and reinforced federal troops already in the state.
Disputed returns left rival gubernatorial claimants, and both parties inaugurated their candidates.

Federal troops left the State House at noon on April 10, 1877.
Correspondence that day arranged transfer of the executive chamber, records, and papers, and the private secretaries formally transferred the office at noon on April 11.
The killings, intimidation, disputed returns, federal decisions, and office transfer establish several mechanisms in sequence; the selected evidence does not assign their relative causal weight.

## comparisons-and-open-questions

Several questions remain separate: county-level electoral effects of intimidation, the practical effect of continued federal protection, the duration of enforcement deterrence, divisions inside the Republican coalition, women's political practice outside the franchise, constitutional access after April 1877, and reconciliation of Hamburg casualty identities and sequence.

Comparison should preserve each institution's setting.
Kahnawà:ke's Type I design uses public hearings to establish a mandate and review drafts before the Council enacts a law.
The Zapatista movement's 2003 design joined municipal delegates in regional councils, while one scholarly account argues that rotating civilian councils coexisted with continuing EZLN political-military authority.
Koto Tinggi's reported structure joins an elected executive, a representative council, and a kin-group customary council, and its formal development meetings include invited women’s-group delegates.
These are bounded comparisons of membership, participation, and layered authority, not historical equivalents or democracy scores.
