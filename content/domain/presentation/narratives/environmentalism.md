Environmentalism names contested ethical commitments, movements, and political projects concerned with human relations to environments and nonhuman life.
It is not environmental science, a single conservation program, one party platform, or a position that can be inferred from Indigenous governance or from support for a particular climate or energy policy.

## meanings

Environmental ethics asks which beings, relationships, and places should matter and why.
Movements turn some of those judgments into collective action, but Guha's global history and Martínez-Alier's attributed “environmentalism of the poor” describe different histories, constituencies, and conflicts rather than stages of one universal movement.

## boundaries

Ecology and environmental science investigate conditions and relationships; they do not by themselves decide what ought to be protected or who should bear costs.
Conservation and preservation concern uses and protection of land and life, while sustainability joins present and future needs without settling whose needs count.
Climate policy overlaps with environmentalism but does not exhaust it, and neither a green party nor a policy label proves environmental purposes or effects.

Nuclear power and nuclear weapons likewise require separate analysis of climate, extraction, land, safety, waste, security, and justice.
Nothing about the environmentalism label supplies a position on either technology.

## justice

Environmental justice asks more than where harm is distributed.
Schlosberg distinguishes distribution from participation and recognition: who receives risks and benefits, who can shape decisions, and whose standing and experience institutions acknowledge.
These questions also expose how conservation can dispossess people when protected-land or forestry rules exclude customary users and authorities.

Indigenous knowledge and land relations are not raw inputs that environmental organizations may detach from sovereignty, responsibility, and colonial history.
Where an Indigenous legal or governance order appears here, its own account and institutional boundaries control the description.

## chipko

In Garhwal during the 1970s, villagers used collective presence and nonviolent obstruction against contracted tree felling amid disputes over state forestry, commercial use, and local livelihood.
The Chandi Prasad Bhatt archive preserves Hindi correspondence and Dasholi Gram Swarajya Mandal files from that organizing context, although the public catalogue, not unpublished records, supports the claim here.
Women participated under gendered divisions of labor, property, subsistence, and authority.
Agarwal therefore challenges explanations based on an innate women–nature bond, while Rangan contests a simple opposition between a unitary state and a unitary village community.

These rival accounts make Chipko analytically useful without turning selected actions into a definition of Indian environmentalism or rural women's politics.

## warren-county

North Carolina placed a PCB-contaminated-soil landfill in a predominantly Black Warren County community.
Residents and allied civil-rights and religious organizations protested in 1982; hundreds were arrested, but the landfill was built and filled.

The General Accounting Office then reported racial and income patterns across four southeastern landfill sites, and the United Church of Christ's national study identified race as a major siting correlate.
The 1991 summit's seventeen-point platform widened the frame again: ecological questions were inseparable from community power, public health, labor, and racism.
That sequence shows movement articulation and institutional response, not proof that Warren County alone caused every later policy change.

## te-awa

The 2017 Te Awa Tupua Act recognized Te Awa Tupua as an indivisible living whole and a legal person.
It established Te Pou Tupua to act as the river's human face and placed Tupua te Kawa within the statutory framework.

Te Pou Tupua describes those values as practised by Whanganui hapū and iwi and emphasizes their continuing authority to speak for their interests.
This is an iwi–Crown settlement and a specific legal-governance arrangement—not an instance of environmentalism imposed on Whanganui relationships from outside.

## comparisons

Compare livelihood, wilderness protection, eco-efficiency, and justice as distinct aims rather than treating them as synonyms.
Then separate attributed purposes from institutional design and observed outcomes, and ask who defines the problem, who decides, whose knowledge is used, who bears costs, and who can contest the result.
