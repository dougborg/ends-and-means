Democracy can name an ideal of political equality, a way to make decisions, an institutional arrangement, or a measured feature of a political system.
No vote, constitution, or public label establishes all of those meanings by itself.

## meanings

Some arguments justify democracy because people subject to collective decisions should stand as political equals.
Other uses identify procedures or institutions, including voting and representation.
The same word therefore carries normative, procedural, institutional, and empirical claims that must be examined separately.

## institutions

Electoral representation authorizes officeholders to exercise bounded public power.
Sortition instead selects participants by lot, often for a defined deliberative task.
Neither mechanism alone answers who is included or how much authority participants hold.

## measurement

International IDEA constructs separate attributes and indicators rather than deriving democratic performance from a country's constitutional name.
Any measure therefore reflects choices about which features count and how evidence is combined.

## bounded-practice

Kahnawà:ke's Community Decision Making Process joins community-specific Haudenosaunee principles to elected-council and legislative institutions.
That bounded example widens institutional comparison without turning one Kanien’kehá:ka process into a universal model of Indigenous democracy.

## disputes

Majority authorization can conflict with equal standing and basic rights.
Disagreement therefore concerns both who decides and which decisions democratic authority may legitimately make.

## comparisons

A republic is one neighboring constitutional idea, not a synonym for democracy.
India's Constitution illustrates the distinction by separately naming a democratic republic and establishing adult-suffrage elections.
