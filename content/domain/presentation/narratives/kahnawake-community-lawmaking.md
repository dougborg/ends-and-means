Kahnawà:ke’s law-making process combines community hearings and consensus with an elected council and legislative commission.
It is a specific, evolving arrangement shaped by Kanien’kehá:ka political commitments and Canadian colonial law—not an example of one universal “tribal” system.

## whose-terms-describe-the-community

Kahnawà:ke’s process begins with the community’s own political language.
Its preamble identifies Kahnawa’kehró:non as part of the Rotinonhsón:ni Confederacy and asserts responsibility for their own laws and affairs.
Canada’s Indian Act uses *band* differently: it is a statutory category tied to federal decisions about land, money, and administration.
Neither term can substitute for the other, and neither identifies one universal form of Indigenous government.

## how-does-the-process-work

Kahnawà:ke approved the process in 2005 to widen direct participation in law-making.
Horn-Miller’s 2013 account defines consensus as responsive deliberation rather than unanimity, with public review before Council enactment.
In January 2024, the Legislative Commission required pre-registration and ten participants and allowed advancement after two under-attended attempts; in April it instead required a drafting-committee recommendation and commission decision on next steps.
Its process page says revisions remain underway, so these notices do not establish every rule now in force.
For sector-specific or administrative laws, Council sets the mandate before consultation and public review.

## is-this-simply-traditional-government

Horn-Miller calls the process a bridge rather than an unchanged survival from the past.
It adapts Haudenosaunee principles inside institutions that also include an elected council, a legislative commission, legal staff, and procedures developed under pressure from Canadian law.
That combination makes labels such as *traditional*, *band council*, *clan government*, or *tribal government* incomplete when used alone.
Horn-Miller also reports disagreement within the community about the authority of the elected council and traditionalists, so the institutional combination should not be mistaken for settled legitimacy.

## what-do-we-know-about-practice

The process creates formal opportunities for community members to propose laws, attend hearings, review drafts, and raise objections.
Actual participation is more limited: 161 of 480 respondents to a 2023 community survey reported attending at least one session or activity.
The commissioned analysis found recurring concerns about the time required and the number of people taking part.
The survey cannot establish a representative participation rate because its collection methods and geographic coverage were not uniform.

## what-does-this-case-establish

This case shows how one community has combined several sources of authority and revised its law-making procedures over time, including a documented hearing-rule change in 2024.
It does not show that Indigenous, kinship-based, confederated, or non-state communities share one structure or occupy a stage before state government.
Useful comparison must instead ask specific questions about participation, representation, jurisdiction, accountability, territory, and relationships with surrounding states.
