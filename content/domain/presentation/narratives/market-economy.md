A market economy uses price-mediated exchange to coordinate a substantial share of production or allocation, but no economy is made of markets alone.
Firms, households, associations, law, public authority, planning, and redistribution shape what can be exchanged, by whom, and on what terms.

## what-is-a-market-economy

A market is an institution for recurring exchange, not just any transfer between two people.
Calling an economy market-based says that prices and exchange coordinate important activities, but it does not tell us who owns productive assets or how extensively public rules and provision operate.
Market economies can therefore include several ownership forms and allocation mechanisms.
The label does not by itself mean capitalism or laissez-faire.

## what-makes-markets-work

Markets depend on rules that establish property claims, contracts, money, competition, and remedies.
Those rules distribute distinct powers, protections, duties, and liabilities rather than one indivisible thing called ownership.
Firms participate in markets but often coordinate work internally through managerial authority.
Adam Smith connected exchange to specialization, yet even his account makes the extent of the market a condition that varies rather than a complete description of an economy.

## how-markets-differ-from-capitalism

Markets predate modern capitalism and can operate under private, public, cooperative, social, or mixed ownership.
Capitalism is usually defined through a wider configuration that may include productive property, wage employment, firms, commodity production, finance, and accumulation.
None of those features alone is enough: paid work and commodity exchange both appeared under other historical arrangements.
The two concepts overlap, but treating them as synonyms hides differences in ownership, authority, and investment.

## what-do-bounded-cases-show

Agrarian England supplies a contested account of how tenancy, class relations, and market dependence changed together rather than proof that trade automatically produced capitalism.
In the Gold Coast, African smallholders shifted resources into cocoa for export under colonial conditions, combining commodity markets with landholding, family labor, hired labor, and migration.
In China after 1978, market entry and competition expanded while plans and state or collective ownership claims persisted.
The cases differ sharply, but each demonstrates that market growth must be analyzed alongside institutions that a price alone cannot reveal.

## what-market-labels-leave-open

The label *market economy* leaves open the ownership of firms, the organization of work, access to finance, the distribution of income and wealth, and the reach of public provision.
It also leaves open which goods may be traded and whether buyers and sellers possess comparable bargaining power.
Regulation is not evidence that markets have disappeared, just as exchange is not evidence that public authority has withdrawn.

## how-to-compare-market-economies

Begin with the actual markets involved and identify their participants, rules, prices, and power differences.
Then examine ownership and control, employment and unpaid work, firm governance, finance, public provision, planning, and redistribution.
That evidence can show how economies differ without forcing each country into a pure market or plan category.
