Monarchy is a governmental form organized around a continuing monarchic office, but the crown alone does not tell us who succeeds, who governs, or who can hold power to account.
Hereditary, elective, rotational, designated, and disputed succession must be distinguished, as must an office's legal powers, delegated acts, reserve powers, civic functions, and influence in practice.

## meanings

The office is not the officeholder, and monarchy is not the same thing as monarchism, a dynasty's tradition, a government's self-description, or an external regime classification.
A monarch may be head of state without being head of government.
Constitutional and parliamentary monarchy describes an allocation of offices and responsibility; it does not by itself prove democracy or a powerless sovereign.
Executive dynastic monarchy describes a different configuration, but it also should not be reduced to one person's rule.

## institutions

Succession rules answer who may occupy the office.
Constitutional rules answer which acts the office may perform, whether ministers advise or direct those acts, and whether any discretion is reserved.
Rules in use answer how those arrangements operate, while symbolic functions and personal influence require their own evidence.

## japan

Japan's Constitution calls the Tennō (天皇), rendered “Emperor” in the government's non-authoritative English reference translation, the symbol of the State and the unity of the people.
It denies the Emperor governmental powers, requires Cabinet advice and approval for acts in matters of state, and enumerates those acts.
The Imperial House Act separately restricts succession to male offspring in the male line.
The House of Representatives commission digest records Professor Yokota's view that public acts beyond constitutionally enumerated acts risk expansion or political exploitation; the digest does not establish a hidden constitutional governing power.
That 2004 digest is the latest evidence of practice used in this case, so later continuity is not asserted.

## tonga

The consulted 2013 consolidation specifies a Legislative Assembly with seventeen people's representatives and nine nobles' representatives, alongside Cabinet members, but that text alone does not establish a pre-reform comparison or democratic outcome.
An official Crown Law release records that in December 2010 the King appointed Lord Tu'ivakano prime minister on the Assembly's recommendation; the Constitution also specifies retained royal functions.
The consulted English consolidation should not displace Tongan texts and meanings; how missionary and colonial mediation shaped that record remains an explicit research question.

## saudi-arabia

Saudi Arabia's 1992 Basic Law declares a monarchy, confines succession to the founder's descendants through an allegiance and designation process, and makes the King head of government.
It grounds governmental authority in al-Qurʾān (القرآن, Qur'an) and al-Sunnah (السنة, Sunnah).
Its Article 56 says the King shall be prime minister, while a September 2022 royal order appointed Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman to that office as an express exception.
Herb supplies a bounded analytical definition of dynastic monarchy rather than evidence about every current Saudi office.
Those facts support analysis of religious legal authority and dynastic government; neither “theocracy” nor “authoritarianism” follows from the crown without a separate definition and institutional evidence.

## boundaries

Japan, Tonga, and Saudi Arabia are bounded contrasts drawn from evidence with different cutoffs, not embodiments of monarchy or claims of present-day completeness.
Empire concerns control over dependent territory, colonial rule concerns domination of one people by another, and nobility names a titled social group; none is synonymous with a monarchic head-of-state office.
Each comparison must state which office, succession rule, formal authority, rule in use, and accountability relationship it is comparing.
