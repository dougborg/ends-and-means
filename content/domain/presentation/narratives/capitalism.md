Capitalism is not simply trade, markets, private belongings, or the pursuit of profit.
It is a disputed name for economic orders that combine legally constituted claims over productive assets with firms, employment, commodity production, finance, and investment, while public institutions define and enforce many of those arrangements.

## what-defines-capitalism

There is no uncontested checklist that makes an economy capitalist.
Geoffrey Hodgson emphasizes a combination of legal property, markets, money and finance, firms, and widespread employment.
Karl Marx instead asks how contractual exchange connects people who control productive resources to people who must sell their capacity to work, and how production reproduces that relation.
Karl Polanyi draws attention to the political construction of markets in land, labor, and money.
These definitions overlap, but they ask different questions and should remain attributed rather than blended into a false consensus.

## which-institutions-work-together

Property is a legal relation among people, not merely possession of a thing, and its operative rights can be divided among different holders.
Employment places work under an employer’s authority for wages, while commodity production directs output toward exchange.
Firms coordinate much of their internal activity administratively rather than through market transactions.
Finance converts legal claims and expected returns into funding for investment.
Law and public authority help constitute property, contracts, organizations, money, and remedies; they are not external additions to an otherwise self-sufficient market.

## why-markets-are-not-enough

Markets and paid labor existed long before the institutional transformation commonly associated with modern capitalism.
Markets can also allocate goods produced under public, cooperative, social, household, or customary ownership.
Commodity production tells us that output is made for exchange, but not who owns productive assets, governs firms, directs work, or finances investment.
Calling every market arrangement capitalist would erase the very institutional differences the label is meant to help compare.

## what-do-bounded-cases-show

Robert Brenner interprets agrarian England through changing relations among landlords, competitive tenants, and laborers, but the causes and timing remain disputed.
Gold Coast cocoa expansion joined African smallholder initiative and varied labor arrangements to colonial government and export markets rather than following one plantation-firm template.
China’s reforms after 1978 expanded markets and enterprise competition while plans, state organizations, and locally or collectively claimed enterprises remained important.
Together, these cases show why evidence about ownership, labor, coordination, finance, and public power must be gathered separately.

## what-remains-disputed

Definitions disagree about which combination of institutions is necessary and about where capitalism begins historically.
The Brenner debate contests whether agrarian class relations, population, trade, political institutions, or regional conditions should carry the most explanatory weight in England.
China’s reforms raise a different boundary problem because more market exchange did not arrive through one uniform change in ownership.
These disputes cannot be settled by assigning a timeless capitalist identity to a country.

## how-to-compare-capitalism

Compare particular rights over productive assets, the authority created by employment, governance inside firms, access to finance, and the rules that organize markets.
Then ask how public, cooperative, household, and customary institutions interact with those arrangements in a specified place and period.
This approach permits comparison among capitalist configurations without treating capitalism as an ideology, an endorsement, or a complete explanation of outcomes.
