In Jinst sum during 1990–1997, herder households coordinated seasonal pasture, campsites, water, and labor through overlapping local relationships after Mongolia’s livestock collectives ended.
Their practices joined reciprocal access and mobile camps to state land ownership and district administration rather than forming a single “nomadic government” type.

## whose-terms-and-boundaries-apply

*Sum* and *bag* named state administrative territories, while *khot ail* described small and changeable household camps.
Some herders identified as *neg nutgiinkhan*, people of one place, but scholars disputed whether that term always named a durable institution.
These terms overlap without naming the same people, authority, or territory.

## what-changed-after-the-collectives

After collective dissolution and livestock privatization, households took responsibility for movement, transport, labor, veterinary inputs, marketing, and risk.
Pasture remained state-owned for common use within administrative units, while collective allocation and transport support disappeared.
The transition changed the institutions coordinating mobility rather than ending pastoral movement.

## how-were-resources-coordinated

Khot ail households pooled animals into species herds and shared work, although camp membership could change by season or year.
Campsite claims could rest on inheritance, customary use, collective-period use, occupancy, or a privately owned shelter.
Winter pasture, summer pasture, campsites, shelters, springs, and wells carried different and sometimes overlapping access rules.

## why-were-boundaries-permeable

Winter and spring use was strongest around a camp and weakened with distance, so neighboring pasture areas overlapped.
Herders described reserving winter and spring forage while granting emergency access during drought or severe winter in expectation of reciprocity.
That flexibility was organized access, not proof that every resource was open to everyone.

## whose-access-was-secure

Poorer and newer herders often negotiated temporary access through kin, acquaintances, or labor exchange.
Established and wealthier households could assert stronger inherited or birthright claims.
The evidence therefore warns that formalizing the visible claimant could convert unequal bargaining power into a harder legal boundary.

## what-does-the-evidence-not-show

The article combines participant observation, interviews, and a survey across Jinst and Bayan-Ovoo, so pooled findings do not automatically describe Jinst alone.
Its institutional recommendation—to test participatory coordination of seasonal movement—was a proposal, not an observed Jinst outcome.
A later multi-ecozone study supports caution about one-size rules but cannot show what happened in Jinst after this episode.

## how-should-this-case-be-compared

Jinst household pasture coordination differed in scale, leadership, evidence, and state relationship from Ruwalla cross-border shaykhly representation.
Both cases make mobility politically important, but that shared feature does not make them one pastoral, nomadic, customary, or stateless type.
Useful comparison keeps each resource, institution, place, and period visible.
