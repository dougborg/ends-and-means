# Comparing Systems by Crux — v2

Eight system families compared across fourteen cruxes. Each crux is a problem every system must solve. For each, the table gives the mechanism each system uses and where it breaks or is unproven. A summary matrix and observations follow.

## How to read this

**The eight systems**

1. **Laissez-faire capitalism** — private ownership, minimal state (defense, courts, contract enforcement).
2. **Social-democratic capitalism** — private ownership and markets plus a large tax-funded state correcting failures and redistributing. Nordic model; the German variant adds codetermination.
3. **Market socialism / economic democracy** — worker-managed or socially-owned firms competing in markets. Schweickart's model, Yugoslav self-management, Mondragón-style cooperative federations.
4. **Central planning** — state ownership and administrative allocation without market prices. Soviet-type; computational planning is the modern variant.
5. **Social anarchism** — no state; production by federated worker associations or communes. Mutualism, collectivism, syndicalism, anarcho-communism. Spain 1936–39 is the main test; Ostrom's polycentric commons the closest documented small-scale analogue.
6. **State capitalism / developmental state** — markets and private firms, with the state steering credit, protecting strategic sectors, and owning key enterprises. Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan; China since 1978.
7. **Anarcho-capitalism** — no state; law, courts, and defense supplied by competing private agencies (Rothbard, David Friedman). No real-world case; medieval Iceland and Somalia 1991–2006 are the contested analogies.
8. **Participatory economics (Parecon)** — no markets, no state; worker and consumer councils, balanced job complexes, pay by effort, allocation by iterated participatory planning with indicative prices (Albert, Hahnel). No real-world case.

**Verdicts** are evidence-weighted judgments. *Untested* means no large-scale, long-duration case exists; it is not the same as *failed*. Cruxes 3, 6, and 9 involve value trade-offs as well as evidence.

## Crux 1 — Information and coordination

*How does the system know what to produce and where resources should go?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Prices aggregate dispersed knowledge; profit and loss steer resources (Hayek). | Prices omit externalities; no mechanism for public goods; monopoly and information asymmetry distort the signal. |
| Social democracy | Same price mechanism; the state corrects known distortions and supplies public goods directly. | Correction is only as good as the regulator; heavily regulated sectors can lose the signal. |
| Market socialism | Price mechanism for goods. Investment allocation is partly administrative (public banks). | The investment side reintroduces a planning problem; banks must judge projects without a capital market's signal. |
| Central planning | Input-output tables, quotas, administrative allocation; modern variant runs optimization on large datasets. | The calculation problem: tacit and real-time knowledge cannot be centrally collected; quota incentives corrupt reported data. Computation addresses volume, not knowledge that only exists once markets generate it. |
| Social anarchism | Mutualism: markets plus mutual credit. Non-market variants: federated councils negotiate directly. | Mutualism inherits the market's gaps; non-market variants face the calculation problem beyond face-to-face scale. Untested long-run. |
| State capitalism | Markets for most goods; the state directs credit and investment into targeted sectors via state banks and industrial policy. | Works for catch-up (copying a known frontier); picking winners degrades at the frontier (Japan after 1990). State-owned enterprises and local-government investment misallocate (China's property and infrastructure debt). |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Pure price system, extended to law, courts, and security as purchasable services. | No mechanism at all for public goods or diffuse externalities; assumes courts and defense behave like ordinary goods. |
| Parecon | Councils submit consumption and production proposals; facilitation boards compute indicative prices; several rounds of revision converge on a plan. | Never tested. Critics (Schweickart, "Nonsense on Stilts") argue the iteration burden is enormous and indicative prices without real trades face the same calculation problem. Hahnel has only simulations. |

**Verdict:** Laissez-faire, social democracy, state capitalism (for catch-up) — strong. Market socialism — strong on goods, weak on investment. Central planning — weak, with a strong negative record. Social anarchism — strong in mutualist form, untested otherwise. Anarcho-capitalism — strong on private goods, no answer for public ones. Parecon — untested; theoretically contested.

## Crux 2 — Innovation, risk, and failure

*Who funds experiments, who bears risk, and what happens when something fails?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Private risk capital; competition selects; bankruptcy clears failures fast. | Short-termism; under-investment in basic research; winners entrench. |
| Social democracy | Same, plus public basic research and social insurance lowering the personal cost of failure. | Too-big-to-fail bailouts soften the budget constraint; public R&D can be captured. |
| Market socialism | Firms compete and can fail; public banks fund new ventures. | Cooperatives have poor access to outside risk capital; federation solidarity funds recreate the soft budget constraint (Fagor, 2013). |
| Central planning | Directed investment into state priorities; failure is administrative. | Strong in a few prioritized sectors; stagnant elsewhere; failure is hidden, not cleared (Kornai). |
| Social anarchism | Cooperative credit or communal funding; failure borne by the association. | No scaled evidence; unclear who bears enough concentrated risk for large, long-horizon bets. |
| State capitalism | Massive directed investment plus private competition in non-strategic sectors; export discipline (firms must compete abroad to keep support). | Excellent for catch-up; frontier innovation is contested — recent Chinese success in EVs, batteries, and solar cuts against the older view that it cannot reach the frontier. Zombie state enterprises persist. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Maximal private risk-bearing; no bailouts of any kind. | No public research at all; same entrenchment problem as laissez-faire with no antitrust remedy. |
| Parecon | Councils propose investments; workers are paid for effort, not output. | No risk-bearer; innovation is a collective proposal with no reward for success. Albert addresses this in a chapter; there is no evidence either way. |

**Verdict:** Laissez-faire, social democracy — strong. State capitalism — strong for catch-up, contested at the frontier. Market socialism — mixed. Central planning — weak outside priorities. Social anarchism, anarcho-capitalism, Parecon — untested.

## Crux 3 — Ownership of production and control of surplus

*Who owns productive assets, who captures profit, and what is the status of wage labor?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Private ownership; surplus to owners; wage labor as voluntary contract. | The socialist critique: the wage relation is hierarchical, and surplus accrues to capital regardless of contribution. |
| Social democracy | Private ownership; surplus partly redirected by taxation; codetermination gives voice without ownership. | Codetermination's measured effects are neutral-to-small-positive; the wage relation is untouched. |
| Market socialism | Workers own or control the firm; profit distributed to members. | Two-tier workforces in practice (a third of Mondragón's workers are members); members resist new hires. |
| Central planning | State ownership; workers are employees of the state. | The state becomes the new owning class (Bakunin's prediction). |
| Social anarchism | Collective or communal ownership; no wage labor; distribution by contribution or need. | Firm-level ownership still fragments production into entitled groups (the anarcho-communist objection). |
| State capitalism | Mixed: private firms, state enterprises, and hybrids; surplus split between private owners and the state; wage labor throughout. | In China the party retains control rights over large private firms regardless of formal ownership; in Korea, chaebol families control conglomerates through minority stakes. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Absolute private ownership including of law and security; wage labor as contract. | Identical to laissez-faire on this crux; removes even the minimal state's constraints on ownership. |
| Parecon | Social ownership; no wage labor; remuneration by effort and sacrifice, measured by co-workers. | Effort is hard to measure and easy to game; balanced job complexes require rotating people out of tasks they are best at. |

**Verdict:** Partly a value question. Market socialism, social anarchism, and Parecon eliminate the wage relation; social democracy moderates it; the others do not.

## Crux 4 — Hierarchy and voice inside the workplace

*Who has authority over work, and how are decisions made?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Managerial hierarchy answerable to owners; voice is exit or individual bargaining. | Firms are internally command-and-control ("islands of tyranny"). |
| Social democracy | Hierarchy plus unions, sectoral bargaining, works councils, board seats. | Voice can be outvoted; union density has fallen outside the Nordics. |
| Market socialism | One member, one vote; elected managers. | Larger cooperatives drift toward conventional hierarchy; Mondragón splits cooperatives around 400–500 workers. |
| Central planning | State-appointed management; nominal councils. | Rigid hierarchy with political oversight; dissent is dangerous. |
| Social anarchism | Direct democracy; recallable delegates; bottom-up federation. | Slow, and thins as groups grow. |
| State capitalism | Managerial hierarchy; unions are state-controlled or party-aligned (China's ACFTU; Singapore's NTUC). | Voice is weakest of any market system; strikes are suppressed or channeled. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Hierarchy by contract; exit only. | Same as laissez-faire, without even labor law. |
| Parecon | Balanced job complexes: every worker does a mix of empowering and rote tasks so no "coordinator class" forms; councils decide. | Its signature idea and its most criticized: rotating experts out of expert work trades competence for equality. Untested. |

**Verdict:** Parecon, social anarchism, market socialism — strongest by design, all scale-limited. Social democracy — moderate. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism, central planning, state capitalism — weak.

## Crux 5 — Externalities and public goods

*How are costs and benefits not captured by prices handled?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Coasean bargaining, property rights, private provision. | Bargaining fails for diffuse harms; public goods under-supplied. |
| Social democracy | Pigouvian taxes, cap-and-trade, regulation, direct provision. | Regulators can be captured; correction lags. Still the best record. |
| Market socialism | Same tools applied to worker-owned firms. | Co-ops have no inherent incentive to internalize externalities; need the same external correction. |
| Central planning | Planners can include any cost in principle. | Worst environmental record of the 20th century in practice (Aral Sea, Soviet-bloc pollution). |
| Social anarchism | Communal ownership aligns incentives locally (Ostrom). | No mechanism for cross-community externalities or continental public goods. |
| State capitalism | The state can act decisively when it prioritizes the problem. | Acts only when it chooses to: China's air pollution turnaround after 2013 followed three decades of growth-first neglect. No independent check forces the choice. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Bargaining and private litigation only. | No coercive remedy for diffuse harms; the weakest column on this crux. |
| Parecon | Consumer councils weigh social and environmental impacts in the plan by construction. | Untested; depends on councils correctly valuing harms with no price benchmark. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest record. State capitalism — strong when willing, unaccountable when not. Market socialism — inherits social democracy's tools. Central planning — weak in practice. Social anarchism — local only. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism — weak. Parecon — untested.

## Crux 6 — Inequality and concentration of private capital

*What stops accumulation from becoming dynastic wealth and political power?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Competition erodes rents; inheritance disperses fortunes. | r > g (Piketty): concentration is the default; network effects create durable monopolies. |
| Social democracy | Progressive taxation, inheritance tax, antitrust, codetermination as pre-distribution. | Wealth taxes mostly repealed in Europe; capital mobility disciplines the taxing state; concentration rose across the OECD after 1980. |
| Market socialism | Structural: no private ownership of large capital. | Concentration relocates to successful cooperatives and public investment banks. |
| Central planning | Structural: no private capital. | Inequality reappears as political privilege (nomenklatura). |
| Social anarchism | Structural: no capital ownership, no state. | Nothing prevents re-concentration except vigilance; no case has run long enough to observe drift. |
| State capitalism | The state can break private concentration at will (Alibaba/Ant, 2020) and owns the commanding heights. | Concentration sits in the party-state and connected families; crony capitalism (chaebol) is the characteristic failure; the state's own power is unchecked. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Nothing structural; competition among protection agencies is supposed to prevent domination. | Nozick's argument: a dominant protection agency becomes a de facto state. Concentration of coercive capacity is the specific risk. |
| Parecon | Structural: no capital; balanced job complexes target the "coordinator class" — the technocratic elite that anarchism and Marxism both missed. | Untested; the coordinator-class analysis is Albert's most distinctive contribution and his answer to why Soviet planning produced a new elite. |

**Verdict:** The structural solutions (market socialism, central planning, anarchism, Parecon) relocate concentration rather than eliminate it. Social democracy — mixed, erodes. State capitalism — concentrates in the state. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism — weak.

## Crux 7 — Concentration of state power and capture of the state

*What constrains the state, and what prevents the powerful from capturing it?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | A small state has little to capture; constitutional limits. | A weak state is captured by default — it cannot enforce against concentrated private power. |
| Social democracy | Separation of powers, courts, free press, unions as counter-power, transparency. Taxation itself creates an accountability bargain. | Regulatory capture (Stigler); discretionary spending enables patronage; durability depends on continual re-contestation. |
| Market socialism | Same tools; fewer concentrated private actors. | Public investment banks are the new capture target (Yugoslav banks were politically directed). |
| Central planning | None internal; the state is the economy. | Worst case: no separation of economic and political power; no press or courts. |
| Social anarchism | No state to capture; recallable delegates; polycentric governance. | The crux reappears as defense and re-emergence; nothing structurally prevents a coercive organization from re-forming. |
| State capitalism | Varies entirely with elite discipline: Singapore's low corruption and meritocratic bureaucracy vs. Marcos-era Philippines. Korea and Taiwan later democratized. | State and capital are fused by design; capture runs both ways. Where elites are undisciplined the result is kleptocracy. No internal mechanism guarantees the Singapore outcome. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | No state to capture. | Absent Leviathan: private domination fills the space; protection agencies become warlords or a state (Somalia 1991–2006 is the contested case — Leeson argues outcomes improved vs. the prior regime; others point to warlord rule). |
| Parecon | No state; facilitation boards and councils. | Untested; facilitation boards are the obvious capture target and the coordinator-class problem in miniature. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest record, capture unresolved. State capitalism — depends on elite discipline, no guarantee. Anarchism and anarcho-capitalism — remove the target, no answer to re-emergence. Central planning — worst. Laissez-faire — weak by omission. Parecon — untested.

## Crux 8 — Labor mobility and exit

*Can people leave a firm or jurisdiction, and how do labor markets clear?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Free labor market. | Non-competes, employer-tied benefits, monopsony. |
| Social democracy | Free labor market plus portable benefits; Nordic "flexicurity." | Insider/outsider markets where employment protection is high (Southern Europe). |
| Market socialism | Workers choose firms but must be admitted; capital accounts pay out slowly; federations reassign internally. | Admission friction, golden handcuffs, mobility bounded by the federation. |
| Central planning | Administrative assignment; internal passports. | Workers often could not legally change cities; emigration criminalized. |
| Social anarchism | Free association. | Admission-dependent; same dilution logic as cooperatives. |
| State capitalism | Labor markets mostly free; heavy reliance on migrant and guest labor. | China's hukou system restricts internal migration and excludes migrants from urban services; Singapore's guest-worker regime ties residence to employer. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Free by contract. | Same as laissez-faire; contracts can restrict exit with no labor law to limit them. |
| Parecon | Council membership; job complexes assigned within councils. | Movement between councils requires re-balancing job complexes; friction likely. Untested. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism — free formally, uneven in practice. State capitalism — free for citizens, restricted for migrants. Market socialism, social anarchism — admission-dependent. Central planning — worst. Parecon — untested.

## Crux 9 — Provision of basic needs and security

*How are healthcare, education, housing, and income floors provided?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Market provision, charity, insurance. | Systematic under-provision for those who cannot pay; adverse selection. |
| Social democracy | Universal tax-funded provision plus social insurance. | Cost growth; the state becomes a large provider (the discretion concern). Strongest outcome record. |
| Market socialism | Assumes a social-democratic welfare state on top. | Adds nothing beyond social democracy here. |
| Central planning | Universal state provision by construction. | Chronic shortage: guaranteed on paper, unreliable in practice; famine when the plan fails. |
| Social anarchism | Communal provision; mutual aid societies. | Community scale only. |
| State capitalism | Targeted, often through mandatory savings (Singapore's CPF) and state housing (HDB houses ~80% of Singaporeans); China expanded basic insurance after 2003. | Korea and Taiwan built welfare states only after democratizing; China's provision is thin outside cities. Coverage follows state priority, not citizen entitlement. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Charity and insurance only. | Weakest column; no mechanism for those who cannot pay. |
| Parecon | Consumption councils allocate by need within the plan. | Untested. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest. State capitalism — effective where prioritized (Singapore housing), thin elsewhere. Central planning — unreliable. Market socialism — inherits social democracy. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism — weak. Social anarchism — local. Parecon — untested.

## Crux 10 — Track record

| System | Evidence base | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | 19th-century Britain and U.S.; no modern pure case. | Rapid growth alongside severe inequality and instability; every modern economy moved away from it. |
| Social democracy | Nordics since the 1930s; Germany since 1951; most of the OECD partially. | Consistently high on freedom, health, mobility, innovation. Transferability contested. |
| Market socialism | Yugoslavia 1950–90; Mondragón since 1956; thousands of cooperatives. | Co-ops match conventional productivity; Yugoslavia collapsed with its state; Mondragón reverts to capitalism abroad. No national case outlasting its regime. |
| Central planning | USSR 1928–91; China 1949–78; Eastern bloc; Cuba; North Korea. | Industrialization in priorities; shortage, stagnation, famine; every case liberalized or collapsed. |
| Social anarchism | Spain 1936–39; Ukraine 1918–21; Rojava since 2012; Ostrom's commons; Zapatistas. | Functional locally under wartime conditions; destroyed externally each time; no peacetime long-run test. |
| State capitalism | Japan 1950–90; Korea, Taiwan, Singapore since the 1960s; China since 1978. | The strongest growth record of the last sixty years; Korea and Taiwan democratized, Singapore and China did not. The one system on this list whose track record is mostly positive and mostly recent. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | No case. Medieval Iceland (930–1262) and Somalia 1991–2006 as contested analogies. | Iceland's system ended in civil war and Norwegian rule; Somalia's outcome is disputed (Leeson vs. critics). No modern evidence. |
| Parecon | No case. Small collectives (South End Press, Z Magazine). | Nothing beyond a handful of workplaces. |

## Crux 11 — Scale sensitivity

*Does it work at 500 people, 5 million, 500 million?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Prices scale without limit; that is the point of the price mechanism. | Public-goods and externality failures grow with scale (climate). |
| Social democracy | Scales via bureaucracy and law; the Nordics are 5–10 million each, Germany 84 million. | Trust and homogeneity questions at U.S. or Indian scale; the largest social democracies are the most contested. |
| Market socialism | Individual cooperatives cap around 500 (Mondragón splits them); federations reach ~80,000. | No national-scale case; the federation's own size bounds mobility and capital pooling. |
| Central planning | Scales administratively — the USSR planned for 290 million. | Information degrades with every layer of hierarchy; the calculation problem is a scale problem. |
| Social anarchism | Strong at village, factory, and commons scale (Ostrom's cases are hundreds to thousands). | Direct democracy and face-to-face trust do not survive scale; regional coordination in Spain was contested. |
| State capitalism | Scales to 1.4 billion (China) through state capacity. | Local-government incentive distortion at scale (GDP-target gaming, debt); the center loses information the same way planners do. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Unknown; theory assumes competition among agencies at any scale. | No evidence; agency competition at continental scale is speculation. |
| Parecon | Planning rounds are combinatorial: every council's proposal affects every other's. | The iteration burden grows faster than population; the strongest theoretical objection to the model. |

**Verdict:** Laissez-faire, central planning, state capitalism — scale by construction (with information loss). Social democracy — proven to ~100 million. Market socialism, social anarchism — bounded. Anarcho-capitalism, Parecon — unknown, with theoretical reasons for concern.

## Crux 12 — Transition path

*How do you get there from here without a revolution?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Deregulation and retrenchment (Thatcher, Reagan, Pinochet's Chile). | Politically hard to sustain; every attempt stopped well short of the model. |
| Social democracy | Incremental, electoral, proven repeatedly (New Deal, postwar Europe, Nordic reforms). | Can be reversed incrementally too; the path is real but never finished. |
| Market socialism | Schweickart assumes a "landslide election." Wright's "interstitial" strategy: grow the co-op sector until it is dominant. Sweden's 1976 Meidner Plan (wage-earner funds buying into firms) was the serious attempt. | Meidner was defeated politically and dismantled by 1991; co-op share of most economies has stayed small for a century. |
| Central planning | Revolution or conquest in every historical case. | No electoral path exists; the transitions were violent. |
| Social anarchism | Revolution (Spain, Ukraine) or prefiguration (build the new inside the old). | Revolutionary cases were destroyed; prefigurative cases stay small. |
| State capitalism | Arose under authoritarian regimes facing security threats (Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) or after a prior collapse (China). | No democratic society has adopted it deliberately; the path ran through dictatorship. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Secession, seasteading, charter cities, "startup societies." | No path from an existing state; charter-city attempts (Honduras) were reversed. |
| Parecon | Prefigurative: build participatory workplaces and councils. | No path proposed beyond example-setting. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — the only proven peaceful path. Laissez-faire — partial retrenchment only. Market socialism — one serious attempt, defeated. The rest — revolution, dictatorship, or no path.

## Crux 13 — External security and defense

*Who defends the system from outside coercion?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | The minimal state's one uncontested function. | Defense is a public good the model otherwise disclaims; large militaries create the state the model wants to limit. |
| Social democracy | Conventional state defense; alliances (NATO, Nordic cooperation). | Small states depend on alliances; defense spending competes with the welfare state. |
| Market socialism | State defense. | Same as social democracy. |
| Central planning | Strong: the USSR won the Eastern Front and matched the U.S. militarily for forty years. | Militarization consumed the civilian economy; defense was the one sector planning served well and at everything else's expense. |
| Social anarchism | Militias; federated defense. | Spain's militias were absorbed into the Republican army then crushed; Makhno was crushed; Rojava survives on external patrons. The weakest column. |
| State capitalism | Strong: the developmental state is partly a security state (Korea, Taiwan, Singapore all built under existential threat). | Security justification underwrites the authoritarianism. |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Private defense agencies. | The standard objection (Nozick): a successful agency becomes a state; an unsuccessful one loses to a state. |
| Parecon | Unaddressed in the model. | — |

**Verdict:** Central planning and state capitalism — strongest, at civilian cost. Social democracy, market socialism, laissez-faire — conventional. Social anarchism — weakest with the clearest historical record. Anarcho-capitalism — theoretically unstable. Parecon — unaddressed.

## Crux 14 — Robustness to bad actors

*What happens when the people running it are self-interested or malicious?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Competition disciplines firms; a small state has little to abuse. | A weak state cannot stop a private actor who achieves dominance; fraud and coercion go unremedied. |
| Social democracy | Checks and balances, courts, press, elections; the Nordics' record of resisting corruption is the best available. | Capture is slow degradation, not a coup; institutions decay when not defended (the "narrow corridor" is narrow). |
| Market socialism | Cooperative governance: elected, recallable management. | Elected managers entrench like any politician; public investment banks concentrate discretion. |
| Central planning | None above the top. | Stalin and Mao: the worst outcomes in the whole table came from unconstrained leaders inside this system. |
| Social anarchism | Recallable delegates; Ostrom's commons rely on monitoring and graduated sanctions, designed for exactly this at small scale. | No answer to an armed faction at any scale; Spain's own revolution saw anarchists fighting communists in Barcelona (May 1937). |
| State capitalism | Depends entirely on elite discipline and succession; Singapore built institutions to enforce it (high official pay, anti-corruption agency). | The Marcos, Suharto, and post-Soviet cases show the same structure producing kleptocracy; succession is the recurring crisis (China's is unresolved). |
| Anarcho-capitalism | Competing agencies check each other. | A dominant agency faces no check; the model assumes competition persists without an enforcer of competition. |
| Parecon | Balanced job complexes and recallable councils prevent a coordinator class. | Designed against elite formation, not against coercion; unaddressed for armed or fraudulent actors. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest record. Social anarchism — strong at small scale (Ostrom), none at large. State capitalism — high variance, no guarantee. Market socialism — moderate. Laissez-faire, anarcho-capitalism — weak against dominant private actors. Central planning — worst. Parecon — partial by design, untested.

## What the expanded matrix suggests

- **State capitalism is the column that most changes the picture.** It has the best recent growth record in the table, scales to the largest population, and handles defense and catch-up innovation well — while scoring weak or unaccountable on voice, capture, and mobility. It shows that the market-vs-planning axis is not the only one: state capacity and elite discipline matter as much as ownership form. Its lesson is uncomfortable for every other column: the most successful development model of the last sixty years is not any of the ones political theorists usually argue about.
- **The four new cruxes are where the stateless models fail most consistently.** Social anarchism and anarcho-capitalism both remove the state and both have no answer to scale, defense, or a dominant armed actor. Their strongest cruxes (voice, capture) are exactly the ones a state creates problems for; their weakest are the ones a state exists to solve. That is the Narrow Corridor argument restated as a table.
- **Transition is the crux that separates serious proposals from thought experiments.** Only social democracy has a proven peaceful path. Market socialism has one real attempt (Meidner) that was defeated. The rest reached power through revolution or dictatorship, or have no proposed path. This is Sawicky's point: compare alternatives to reachable options, not to the status quo.
- **Social democracy still has no "worst" or "untested" cell**, but the expanded table sharpens its weakness: its strongest cruxes all depend on a large, trusted state, and three of the four new cruxes (scale, transition, bad actors) are about whether that trust and capacity can be built or kept where they do not already exist.
- **Parecon is the most fully designed of the untested models** and the only one that names the coordinator-class problem directly. Its answers on voice and concentration are the strongest on paper; its answers on scale and information are the most contested on paper. It has never been tried beyond a few workplaces, which is the whole difficulty.
- **The value-dominated cruxes remain 3 and 6.** The other twelve are mostly empirical, and the evidence is uneven enough that the honest summary is: two columns (social democracy, state capitalism) have extensive positive records with serious known weaknesses; one (central planning) has an extensive negative record; the rest are bounded, partial, or untested.
