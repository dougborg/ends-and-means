# ADR 0005: Model society-specific political organization before broad labels

**Status:** accepted

## Context

Readers may search for terms such as *tribe*, *band*, *clan*, *chiefdom*, *council*, *confederacy*, *customary law*, or *nomadic government*.
Those words do not name interchangeable political systems.
They may instead be a community self-description, a translation, a colonial or statutory classification, a historical scholarly category, an institution within a larger arrangement, or a livelihood and mobility pattern that says little by itself about government.

Anthropological use of *tribe* and *chiefdom* has also been entangled with unilinear accounts that placed societies on a path from band to tribe to chiefdom to state.
The plural graph cannot encode that sequence without contradicting its rules for bounded evidence, plural classification, and independent institutional relationships.

The Kahnawà:ke Community Decision Making and Review Process exposes the representational boundary.
Community self-description, Canadian legal categories, Haudenosaunee principles, an elected council, a legislative commission, public hearings, and rival claims about authority coexist in one bounded case.
No single broad label represents that combination honestly.

## Decision

Model the named people, place, institution, and period that the evidence supports before assigning a broad political category.

- A community’s own name and political vocabulary remain distinct from translated names and external classifications.
- *Band* is contextual when it records a Canadian statutory category; it is not a universal Concept or synonym for a people, nation, clan, or political system.
- *Tribe* remains a common-language search query and a quoted or attributed historical term unless a named community owns it as a self-description.
  It is not a universal Concept, Collection, or Case type.
- *Clan* may identify a society-specific relationship or institution when community and scholarly sources specify its membership, authority, and scope.
  It does not imply one kinship-based form of government.
- *Council* and *confederacy* may become Concepts, Organizations, or institutional Means only when a concrete research tranche demonstrates which actors, authority, decision rules, scope, and relationships the record needs.
  The English label alone is not enough.
- *Chiefdom* is a historiographic or analytical claim requiring attribution, scope, and rival evidence.
  It is not a stage or default canonical type.
- *Customary law* does not collapse distinct legal orders into one system.
  Name the community’s law, institutions, sources, and relationship to other legal orders.
- *Nomadic* and *pastoral* describe mobility or livelihood only where people use or researchers define those terms precisely.
  Neither term determines authority, property, kinship, territory, or state relationships.

Subject Guide search queries may later route these familiar phrases to reviewed, society-specific destinations without creating a canonical identity for the query.
Until the guide and alias owner exists, the query remains an explicit discovery requirement rather than a hidden alternate label.

## Evidence and community-source rule

Research about living communities must include sources produced within or authorized by the community whenever those sources exist and are appropriate to the claim.
Community records and oral histories are not automatically transparent, unanimous, or public.
Record who speaks, the institutional or personal capacity in which they speak, the permissions and publication context, and the limits of the account.
External scholarship and state records remain necessary when they support different claims, but they cannot silently replace community self-description.

## Consequences

The canonical graph gains a bounded Kahnawà:ke Case and atomic evidence without gaining a universal tribe, band, clan, chiefdom, council, confederacy, customary-law, nomadic, or pastoral system entity.
Tests protect those absences as intentional model boundaries rather than missing content.
Future research can add society-specific entities and relationships without inheriting a rank, stage, or presumed package of institutions.

This decision favors sparse, explainable records over premature comparability.
The cost is that common-language search coverage depends on reviewed Subject Guides and explicit query ownership rather than broad ontology nodes.
