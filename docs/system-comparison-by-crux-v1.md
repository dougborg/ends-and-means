# Comparing Systems by Crux

A framework for comparing how different economic/political systems address the core problems that came up in the political-economy notes. Each crux is a problem every system has to solve somehow. For each, the table shows the mechanism each system uses, and where that mechanism breaks or is unproven.

## How to read this

**The five systems** (each is a family, with variants noted):

1. **Laissez-faire capitalism** — private ownership, minimal state; anarcho-capitalism is the limiting case.
2. **Social-democratic capitalism** — private ownership and markets, plus a large tax-funded state correcting failures and redistributing. Nordic model; the German variant adds codetermination.
3. **Market socialism / economic democracy** — worker-managed or socially-owned firms competing in markets. Schweickart's model, Yugoslav self-management, Mondragón-style cooperative federations.
4. **Central planning** — state ownership and administrative allocation without market prices. Soviet-type; computational-planning proposals (Cockshott & Cottrell) are the modern variant.
5. **Social anarchism** — no state; production organized by federated worker associations or communes. Mutualism, collectivism, syndicalism, anarcho-communism; Spain 1936–39 is the main test; Ostrom's polycentric commons is the closest well-documented small-scale analogue.

**The verdicts** at the end of each table are evidence-weighted judgments, not scores. *Untested* means no large-scale, long-duration case exists — it is not the same as *failed*. Cruxes 6, 7, and 9 involve value trade-offs as well as evidence; the tables try to separate the two.

## Crux 1 — Information and coordination

*How does the system know what to produce, in what quantity, and where resources should go?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Prices aggregate dispersed knowledge; profit and loss steer resources (Hayek). | Prices omit externalities; no mechanism for public goods; information asymmetries and monopoly distort the signal. |
| Social democracy | Same price mechanism, with the state correcting known distortions (Pigouvian taxes, regulation) and supplying public goods directly. | Correction is only as good as the regulator; regulated sectors (healthcare, housing) can lose the signal entirely. |
| Market socialism | Same price mechanism for goods; firms respond to market signals. Investment allocation is partly administrative (Schweickart's public banks). | The investment side reintroduces a planning problem — banks must judge projects without a capital market's signal. Sawicky's critique: "all the problems of central planning persist" on the investment side. |
| Central planning | Input-output tables, quotas, administrative allocation. Modern variant: linear programming on large datasets. | The calculation problem: tacit and real-time knowledge cannot be centrally collected; quota incentives corrupt reported data (Great Leap Forward over-reporting). Computation addresses volume, not the knowledge that only exists once markets generate it. |
| Social anarchism | Mutualism: markets plus mutual credit. Syndicalism/communism: federated councils negotiate production and distribution directly. | Mutualism inherits the market's strengths and gaps. Non-market variants face the calculation problem at any scale beyond face-to-face; no long-run test exists. |

**Verdict:** Laissez-faire and social democracy — strong. Market socialism — strong on goods, weak on investment. Central planning — weak, with a strong historical record of failure. Anarchism — strong in mutualist form, untested in non-market form.

## Crux 2 — Innovation, risk, and failure

*Who funds experiments, who bears the risk, and what happens when something fails?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Private risk capital chases returns; competition selects; bankruptcy clears failures quickly (hard budget constraint, creative destruction). | Short-termism; under-investment in basic research (a public good); winners entrench and suppress later competition. |
| Social democracy | Same private mechanism, plus public funding of basic research and education, and social insurance that lowers the personal cost of failure. | Bailouts of "too big to fail" firms soften the budget constraint; public R&D can be captured or politicized. |
| Market socialism | Firms still compete and can fail. Investment banks fund new ventures from the capital tax. | Cooperatives have harder access to outside risk capital (giving equity to investors conflicts with worker ownership), biasing toward incremental innovation. Federation solidarity funds recreate the soft budget constraint — Mondragón bankrolled Fagor for years before its 2013 bankruptcy. |
| Central planning | Directed investment into state priorities; failure is administrative, not financial. | Strong in a few prioritized sectors (Soviet space, military); stagnant elsewhere. Soft budget constraint is universal (Kornai). Failure is hidden rather than cleared. |
| Social anarchism | Mutualism: cooperative credit funds ventures; failure is borne by the association. Communism: experiments funded by the commune. | No scaled evidence. The open question is whether anyone bears enough concentrated risk to fund large, long-horizon bets. |

**Verdict:** Laissez-faire — strong on selection, weak on basic research. Social democracy — strong overall. Market socialism — mixed; capital access is the documented weakness. Central planning — weak outside prioritized sectors. Anarchism — untested.

## Crux 3 — Ownership of production and control of surplus

*Who owns productive assets, who captures the profit, and what is the status of wage labor?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Private ownership; surplus to owners; wage labor is a voluntary contract. | The socialist critique: wage labor is a hierarchical relation, not a contract between equals; surplus accrues to capital regardless of contribution. |
| Social democracy | Private ownership retained; surplus partly redirected through taxation; codetermination (Germany) gives workers board seats without ownership. | Codetermination's measured effects are neutral-to-small-positive — it moderates rather than transforms. Ownership and the wage relation are untouched. |
| Market socialism | Workers own or control their firm; profit is distributed to members; no fixed wage. Schweickart: society owns capital, workers manage it. | Two-tier workforces emerge in practice (Mondragón: about a third of workers are members; 100+ conventional subsidiaries abroad). Members resist new hires because each dilutes the profit share. |
| Central planning | State ownership; surplus to the state; workers are wage employees of the state. | Bakunin's prediction: the state becomes the new owning class. Workers have no more control than under private ownership, and often less. |
| Social anarchism | Collective or communal ownership; no wage labor; distribution by contribution (collectivism) or need (communism). | Anarcho-communists argue firm-level ownership still fragments production into entitled groups. Personal-vs-productive property lines get contested at the edges (rental housing, small proprietors). |

**Verdict:** This crux is partly a value question. On the narrow point of eliminating the wage relation: market socialism and anarchism do; social democracy moderates it; laissez-faire and central planning do not.

## Crux 4 — Hierarchy and voice inside the workplace

*Who has authority over work, and how are decisions made in the firm?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Managerial hierarchy answerable to owners; workers' voice is exit (quit) or individual bargaining. | Firms are "islands of tyranny" (Chomsky, quoted by Phillips & Rozworski) — internally command-and-control, with voice only at the margin. |
| Social democracy | Hierarchy plus institutional voice: unions, sectoral bargaining, works councils, board representation. | Voice without ownership can be outvoted (German chairman holds the tiebreak); union density has declined in most countries outside the Nordics. |
| Market socialism | One-member-one-vote; managers are elected. | Larger cooperatives delegate to elected management and drift toward conventional hierarchy; Mondragón cooperatives split at ~400–500 workers because democratic solidarity thins beyond that. |
| Central planning | State-appointed management; nominal workers' councils. | In practice a rigid hierarchy with political oversight; voice is minimal and dissent is dangerous. |
| Social anarchism | Direct democracy in assemblies; delegates are recallable; federation from the bottom up. | Scale: direct democracy is slow and thins as groups grow. Spanish collectives worked at village and factory scale; coordination across regions was contested. |

**Verdict:** Anarchism and market socialism — strongest by design; both face a scale problem. Social democracy — moderate, institutionalized. Laissez-faire and central planning — weak.

## Crux 5 — Externalities and public goods

*How are costs and benefits not captured by prices handled — pollution, infrastructure, basic research, defense?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Coasean bargaining and property rights; private provision or under-provision. | Transaction costs make bargaining fail for diffuse harms (carbon); public goods are systematically under-supplied. |
| Social democracy | Pigouvian taxes, cap-and-trade, regulation, direct public provision. | Regulators can be captured; correction is politically contested and often lags. Still the best empirical record. |
| Market socialism | Same tools as social democracy, applied to worker-owned firms; Schweickart adds "socialist protectionism" for trade. | Worker-owned firms have no inherent incentive to internalize externalities — a co-op will pollute to protect members' income. Requires the same external correction. |
| Central planning | Planners can in principle include any cost in the plan. | In practice, the worst environmental record of the 20th century (Aral Sea, Soviet-bloc pollution) — no price discipline, no free press, no courts to hold planners to account. |
| Social anarchism | Communal ownership aligns incentives locally (Ostrom's commons). | Works for bounded local commons; no mechanism for cross-community externalities or continental-scale public goods. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest record. Laissez-faire — weak. Market socialism — needs the same correction as capitalism. Central planning — weak in practice despite theory. Anarchism — strong locally, untested beyond.

## Crux 6 — Inequality and concentration of private capital

*What stops accumulation from becoming dynastic wealth and political power?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Competition erodes rents; inheritance disperses fortunes over generations. | Piketty's r > g: returns to capital outrun growth, so concentration is the default. Network effects and scale create durable monopolies. Concentrated capital funds capture of whatever state exists. |
| Social democracy | Progressive income tax, transfers, inheritance tax, antitrust; codetermination as pre-distribution. | Wealth taxes mostly repealed in Europe over capital flight; capital mobility disciplines the taxing state. The Chicago School narrowed antitrust to prices only. Concentration still rose in most OECD countries since 1980. |
| Market socialism | Structural: no private ownership of large capital, so no capitalist class to concentrate. Schweickart's capital tax funds investment publicly. | Concentration shifts to successful cooperatives and their members; Mondragón's pay ratio widened from ~3:1 to ~6:1. Investment banks become a new locus of power. |
| Central planning | Structural: no private capital at all. | Inequality reappears as political privilege — the nomenklatura, special access, dachas. Concentration of economic and political power in one apparatus. |
| Social anarchism | Structural: no capital ownership, no state to capture. Collectivists cap by contribution; communists by need. | Nothing prevents re-concentration except ongoing collective vigilance; no case has run long enough to observe drift. |

**Verdict:** Market socialism, central planning, and anarchism solve this by construction but relocate concentration elsewhere. Social democracy — mixed and contested; the tools exist but erode. Laissez-faire — weak.

## Crux 7 — Concentration of state power and capture of the state

*What constrains the state, and what prevents the powerful from capturing it?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | A minimal state has little to capture; constitutional limits on government scope. | A weak state is captured *by default* — it cannot enforce against concentrated private power (Acemoglu & Robinson's "absent Leviathan"). Anarcho-capitalism removes the state and leaves private power unconstrained. |
| Social democracy | Separation of powers, independent courts, free press, high union density as counter-power, transparency, campaign-finance rules. Taxation itself creates an accountability bargain (the rentier-state literature in reverse). | Regulatory capture (Stigler) is the unresolved problem. A large discretionary state can buy loyalty through patronage. Durability depends on continual re-contestation. |
| Market socialism | Same constitutional tools; fewer concentrated private actors able to capture. | Public investment banks are a new capture target — who decides which cooperatives get capital, and who watches them? Yugoslav banks were politically directed. |
| Central planning | None internal to the system; the state *is* the economy. | The worst case on this crux: no separation between economic and political power; no independent press or courts; the Great Leap Forward's information suppression is the canonical failure. |
| Social anarchism | Structural: no state to capture. Federation with recallable delegates; polycentric governance (Ostrom). | The crux reappears as *defense and re-emergence*: Spain's collectives were destroyed by Franco within three years; nothing structurally prevents a coercive organization from re-forming. The Narrow Corridor argument: absence of Leviathan is its own failure mode. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest available record, with capture as the live weakness. Anarchism — solves capture by removing the target but has no answer to external or re-emergent coercion. Central planning — worst. Laissez-faire — weak by omission.

## Crux 8 — Labor mobility and exit

*Can people leave a firm or a jurisdiction, and how do labor markets clear?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Free labor market; quit and rehire at will; wages clear supply and demand. | Mobility is formal, not always real: non-competes, employer-tied healthcare, monopsony in thin labor markets. |
| Social democracy | Free labor market plus portable benefits (universal healthcare, pensions) that make exit genuinely available. Nordic "flexicurity": easy to fire, strong safety net. | High employment protection in some variants (Southern Europe) creates insider/outsider labor markets. |
| Market socialism | Workers choose firms, but must be *admitted* by existing members. Capital accounts pay out on exit over years, not instantly. Federations reassign workers internally. | Admission friction (members resist dilution); "golden handcuffs" from capital accounts; mobility is bounded by the federation's size. Two of Mondragón's largest cooperatives left the federation in 2022 to escape solidarity obligations. |
| Central planning | Administrative assignment; internal passports; residence permits. | Soviet workers often could not legally change cities; labor was allocated, not chosen. Exit from the country was criminalized. |
| Social anarchism | Free association; anyone can leave a collective or join another. | Depends on other collectives' willingness to admit; the same dilution logic as cooperatives applies if surplus is shared by contribution. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest (portable benefits make exit real). Laissez-faire — strong formally, uneven in practice. Market socialism — weaker by construction; the documented friction. Central planning — worst. Anarchism — free in principle, admission-dependent in practice.

## Crux 9 — Provision of basic needs and security

*How are healthcare, education, housing, and income floors provided?*

| System | Mechanism | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | Market provision; charity; insurance. | Systematic under-provision for those who cannot pay; adverse selection in insurance markets; the historical record that motivated every other system. |
| Social democracy | Universal, tax-funded provision (healthcare, education, childcare) plus social insurance. | Cost growth; queues; the state becomes a large employer and provider, which is the discretion concern in Crux 7. The strongest outcome record on life expectancy, mobility, poverty. |
| Market socialism | Same universal provision, funded by the capital tax and profits. Schweickart assumes a social-democratic welfare state on top of the ownership change. | Adds nothing beyond social democracy on this crux; the ownership change is orthogonal. |
| Central planning | Universal state provision by construction. | Chronic shortage (Kornai): quantity guaranteed on paper, quality and availability not. Famine when the plan fails. |
| Social anarchism | Communal provision by need (anarcho-communism); mutual aid societies (mutualism). | Historical mutual aid worked at community scale; no evidence for continental-scale healthcare without a state. |

**Verdict:** Social democracy — strongest record. Central planning — universal on paper, unreliable in practice. Market socialism — inherits social democracy's answer. Laissez-faire — weak. Anarchism — strong locally, untested at scale.

## Crux 10 — Track record

*What is the empirical basis for judging the system at all?*

| System | Evidence base | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Laissez-faire | 19th-century Britain and U.S.; Gilded Age; no modern pure case. | Rapid growth and innovation alongside severe inequality and instability; every modern economy has moved away from it. |
| Social democracy | Nordics since the 1930s; Germany since 1951; most of the OECD in partial form. | Consistently high on freedom, health, mobility, and innovation indices. Transferability to large, diverse, poorer countries is contested. |
| Market socialism | Yugoslavia 1950–1990; Mondragón since 1956; thousands of cooperatives; German codetermination as a partial case. | Cooperatives match conventional productivity; Yugoslavia had real growth then collapsed with the state; Mondragón reverts to capitalism when expanding. No national-scale case that outlasted its founding regime. |
| Central planning | USSR 1928–1991; China 1949–1978; Eastern bloc; Cuba, North Korea. | Industrialization in prioritized sectors; chronic shortage, stagnation, environmental damage, and famine; every case liberalized or collapsed. |
| Social anarchism | Spain 1936–39; Ukraine 1918–21 (Makhno); Rojava since 2012; Ostrom's commons cases; Zapatista communities. | Functional at local and regional scale for short periods under wartime conditions; destroyed externally each time; no case has faced a peacetime, long-run test. |

## Summary matrix

| Crux | Laissez-faire | Social democracy | Market socialism | Central planning | Social anarchism |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Information | Strong | Strong | Strong (goods) / Weak (investment) | Weak | Strong (mutualist) / Untested |
| 2. Innovation & failure | Strong / weak on basic research | Strong | Mixed | Weak | Untested |
| 3. Ownership & surplus | Value question | Moderates | Transforms | State replaces owner | Transforms |
| 4. Workplace voice | Weak | Moderate | Strong (scale-limited) | Weak | Strong (scale-limited) |
| 5. Externalities | Weak | Strong | Same as social democracy | Weak in practice | Strong locally |
| 6. Capital concentration | Weak | Mixed, erodes | By construction; relocates | By construction; relocates | By construction; unobserved |
| 7. State power & capture | Weak by omission | Strongest record; capture unresolved | Same + investment banks | Worst | No state; no defense |
| 8. Labor mobility | Strong formally | Strongest | Weaker by design | Worst | Admission-dependent |
| 9. Basic needs | Weak | Strongest | Inherits SD | Unreliable | Local only |
| 10. Evidence | Historical only | Extensive | Partial | Extensive (negative) | Sparse |

## What the matrix suggests

- Every system solves some cruxes by construction and thereby moves the problem rather than eliminating it. Market socialism and anarchism remove the capitalist class and relocate concentration to successful firms or investment banks. Central planning removes private capital and fuses economic and political power.
- Social democracy is the only column without a "worst" or "untested" cell, which is why the conversation kept landing there — but its strongest cruxes (5, 8, 9) depend on a large state, and its weakest crux (7) is precisely the durability of constraints on that state.
- The anarchist column's pattern is consistent: strong wherever the problem is local and face-to-face (voice, commons, mutual aid), untested or undefended wherever scale or external coercion enters. Ostrom's work is the best evidence that the local pattern is real; the Spanish and Ukrainian cases are the best evidence for the scale problem.
- The two cruxes where value judgment dominates evidence are 3 (whether eliminating the wage relation matters in itself) and 6 (how much inequality is acceptable). The rest are mostly empirical.
