# Political Economy Notes: Socialism, Markets, Worker Ownership, and Institutional Design

Summaries of a conversation on comparative economic systems, followed by a curated reading list.

---

## Part 1 — Summaries

### 1. Communism vs. socialism

- **In Marxist theory** they are stages, not rivals. Socialism is the transitional phase: collective/state ownership of production, a state still exists, distribution "according to contribution." Communism is the theorized end-state: stateless, classless, common ownership, distribution "according to need." Lenin formalized this into the two-stage sequence.
- **In practice and common usage** the terms diverged. "Socialism" spans democratic socialism (social ownership via elections, often compatible with markets and private property) to state socialism (centralized control). "Communism" as implemented (USSR, Maoist China, Cuba) meant one-party rule and state control of the economy; the stateless end-state never arrived anywhere.
- **Where they overlap**: both critique private ownership of the means of production. **Where they differ in practice**: socialism is more often compatible with markets, private property, and electoral democracy.

### 2. Social democracy vs. democratic socialism (the screenshot argument)

- The list "Medicaid, Social Security, roads, fire departments, public schools" is **social democracy** (or welfare-state capitalism), not democratic socialism. Social democracy keeps a capitalist market economy and funds services and redistribution through taxation. Democratic socialism seeks to transfer ownership of production to workers or the public.
- The reply "socialism is government ownership of the means of production" is the general definition of socialism, not "state socialism" specifically. Both democratic and authoritarian socialism satisfy it.
- Nuance: fire departments, roads, and public schools are publicly owned infrastructure — closer to real (municipal) socialism than Medicaid or Social Security, which are pure transfer/insurance programs with no ownership component.

### 3. Personal vs. private property

- Socialist theory distinguishes **personal property** (things for individual use: home, car, clothes, savings) from **private property** in the technical sense (capital used to profit from others' labor: factories, large-scale land, rental empires).
- Marx in the *Communist Manifesto*: communism abolishes bourgeois property, not property in general.
- **Contested edges**: a second home rented for income is capital under this framework; small self-employed proprietorship is treated more leniently.
- **Practice diverged**: Soviet collectivization seized peasant land and livestock that functioned as subsistence property, not capital.

### 4. The self-employed workman

- A worker who owns and personally works their own tools, employing no one, does not create the exploitative relation Marx targeted.
- But Marx classified this figure as the **petite bourgeoisie** — a third class, distinct from both bourgeoisie and proletariat — and predicted it would be squeezed out by capitalist concentration, not preserved as a model. He mocked the idea that combining ownership and labor in one person resolved the class struggle.
- Marx's vision was collective ownership of large-scale, already-socialized industry, not millions of small workshops.
- **Mutualism** (Proudhon), anarchist, and guild-socialist traditions, by contrast, build directly on this model.

### 5. Terminology for "workers should own the firms they work in"

- **Worker ownership** — the plainest term for the goal.
- **Worker cooperative** — the institutional form (Mondragón is the canonical example).
- **Workplace / industrial democracy** — emphasizes governance (voting) rather than ownership.
- **Economic democracy** — the broadest umbrella; also the name of David Schweickart's specific market-socialist model.
- **Worker self-management** — the Yugoslav model (state ownership, workers' councils run firms).
- **Syndicalism** — direct worker control of industry via revolutionary unions.

### 6. Compatibility with anarchist philosophies

- **Very high** across the social/left anarchist tradition — worker ownership is near-definitional, not peripheral:
  - Mutualism: worker/artisan possession plus mutual credit.
  - Collectivist anarchism (Bakunin): collective ownership by worker associations, pay by labor; implemented in rural Spain in 1936.
  - Anarcho-syndicalism: unions seize and run production; CNT collectivized industry and land in Catalonia and Aragon, 1936–39.
  - Anarcho-communism (Kropotkin): endorses worker control but goes further.
- **Internal disagreement**: anarcho-communists criticize firm-level worker ownership as insufficient — it fragments production into entitled groups and excludes non-producers. "Communisation" critics call cooperatives competing in markets "self-managed capitalism."
- **The outlier**: anarcho-capitalism does not require worker ownership and accepts wage labor. Most of the historical anarchist tradition rejects its claim to the label precisely because it lacks the "wage slavery" critique.

### 7. Innovation and experimentation without conventional capitalism

- **Market socialism keeps competition and price signals** and changes only who owns the firms. Yugoslavia and Mondragón are real cases. This preserves the innovation mechanism markets are credited with.
- **Fully non-market systems have a weak track record.** The socialist calculation debate (Mises, Hayek) argued prices are an information-processing mechanism no planner can replicate. Soviet-style economies innovated in narrow state-prioritized sectors (space, military) but stagnated across consumer and process innovation.
- **Genuinely unresolved**: decentralized non-market anarchist models (no long-run test at scale — Spain lasted three years in wartime); computational planning (Cockshott & Cottrell) is untested; worker co-ops match conventional firms on productivity but evidence on breakthrough R&D is thinner, partly because co-ops have harder access to outside risk capital.

### 8. A critique of the calculation debate, assessed

- **"Prices ignore externalities"** — true (standard economics since Pigou), but the mainstream fix is to correct prices (Pigouvian taxes, cap-and-trade), not abandon them. Planned economies had some of the worst environmental records of the 20th century (Aral Sea, Soviet-bloc pollution).
- **"Corporations plan internally"** — true; this is Coase's *The Nature of the Firm* (1937). But firms are "islands of planning in a sea of markets": internal planning works because the firm as a whole is disciplined by external competition, capital markets, and bankruptcy. Removing the external market (economy-wide planning) removes the mechanism that makes firm-level planning work. Sears' failed internal-market experiment shows planning can beat internal markets *within* a firm; it says nothing about removing external markets.
- **"Better data and computation"** — the genuinely live question. But Hayek's objection was not primarily computational: much relevant knowledge is tacit, local, and generated in real time by people responding to prices. More computing power addresses volume; it does not obviously conjure information that does not exist prior to the market process.
- **"The famines were just bad ideas"** — the evidence does not support cleanly separating the ideas from the mechanism. A *Journal of Political Economy* analysis attributes 61% of the Great Leap Forward output collapse to central planning's resource diversion and excessive procurement. Local officials facing quotas over-reported harvests; the center kept extracting grain based on false numbers. Information distortion up a quota-driven hierarchy is structural to central planning, not incidental. Democratic accountability and a free press would plausibly have caught it faster — a real distinction between authoritarian and democratic planning proposals.

### 9. Labor markets in economic democracies

- **Schweickart's model**: no traditional labor market — workers choose where to work; firms distribute profit shares rather than fixed wages, so compensation varies by firm performance. Capital is allocated by public investment banks funded by a capital-assets tax; no capital market either.
- **The recruitment friction**: a new member dilutes existing members' profit share, so self-managed firms have a structural incentive to under-hire. A conventional hiring manager bears no such personal cost. This is a standard critique of the model.
- **Mondragón's actual mechanics**: 6–12 month probation as a wage employee; capital buy-in of roughly €15,000–17,000, usually via payroll deduction or a loan from the cooperative bank; membership approved by vote; at least 80% of workers in a cooperative must be members.
- **Mobility on exit**: a member's share of retained surplus sits in an individual capital account, paid out on departure over a schedule rather than instantly — built-in "golden handcuffs." Higher retention, lower fluidity.
- **Competing for talent**: Mondragón's pay ratio drifted from an original ~3:1 to ~6:1 federation-wide (up to 9:1 in some cooperatives) under pressure to attract managers who could earn more at conventional firms.

### 10. The federation model — Mondragón's record

- **What it solves**: internal reassignment of workers from failing to healthy cooperatives; a cooperative bank and solidarity fund that channel surplus into new ventures and struggling members.
- **The tension**: surplus-sharing is a **soft budget constraint** (Kornai) — the same mechanism blamed for socialist inefficiency. The federation bankrolled Fagor Electrodomésticos for years; it went bankrupt anyway in 2013, later and at greater cost, with 3,500+ subsidiary jobs lost.
- **The fracture**: in December 2022, Ulma and Orona — two large, successful cooperatives — voted to leave the federation to stop paying 10% of profits into the solidarity fund.
- **Expansion into new markets happened by reverting to capitalism**: 100+ foreign subsidiaries structured as conventional firms with non-member wage employees. Only about half of Mondragón's businesses are cooperatives and only about a third of its workforce are members. Mondragón cites its own redeployment system as an obstacle to converting foreign plants.

### 11. Landing on regulated capitalism

- The evidence throughout points toward markets for allocation and innovation, with external correction for their failure modes — i.e., **social democracy**, the term from the start of the conversation.
- **Two caveats**: (1) "strong controls and taxation" is where the real argument lives — how much, of what kind, how progressive — not a resolution. (2) **Regulatory capture** (Stigler) is the serious unresolved objection: concentrated capital can shape the rules meant to constrain it, and capital mobility disciplines regulators as much as the reverse.
- Worker cooperatives remain available as one legal form *within* regulated market economies rather than as a replacement for them.

### 12. Strongest contender, from scratch

- The question mixes empirical findings with irreducible value trade-offs (equality vs. growth, decommodification vs. efficiency, centralization vs. capture-resistance). The evidence-supported design principles:
  1. Keep price-mediated competition as the primary coordination mechanism.
  2. Correct market failures from outside (Pigouvian taxes, antitrust, progressive taxation) rather than restructuring ownership.
  3. Give workers structural voice without abolishing capital markets — **German codetermination** (worker representatives fill about half the supervisory board seats at companies over 2,000 employees). Evidence: neutral-to-small-positive effects — no harm to profitability, wages, or employment; modest increases in capital investment and job security. A real institution, not a transformative one.
  4. Treat concentrated power as the central maintenance problem: antitrust as prevention, federalism, transparency, independent institutions.
- **A concrete sketch**: Nordic-style mixed economy + German-style codetermination + unusually aggressive antitrust and anti-capture safeguards. **Its limit**: Nordic countries are small, wealthy, historically homogeneous, and (Norway) oil-rich; transferability is an open empirical question.

### 13. Taxation, tyranny, capture, and concentration

- **Taxation and tyranny**: the empirical record runs mostly the other way. The highest-tax countries (Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway, Belgium, France) are among the freest. States that don't need to tax their citizens — **rentier states** funded by oil or aid — are the ones that tend toward unaccountability, because taxation forces a consent bargain. The surviving kernel of the worry is **discretion**: patronage-capable spending and state control of economic life, not the revenue level. Rule-bound universal programs are harder to weaponize than discretionary grants.
- **The core dilemma** — a strong state is needed to constrain private power but can dominate society — is Acemoglu & Robinson's *Narrow Corridor*: the answer is a strong state matched by a strong, organized society. Madison, Federalist 51: ambition made to counteract ambition.
- **Prevention over cure** (Hacker's "pre-distribution"): structure markets so concentration doesn't form, which also shrinks the redistributive state.
- **Anti-concentration and anti-capture toolkit**:
  1. Antitrust as the front line (the Chicago School's consumer-welfare narrowing vs. the neo-Brandeisian revival — Khan, Wu).
  2. Tax rent, not production, and tax what can't flee: land value tax (Friedman's "least bad tax"); inheritance tax for dynastic concentration. Net wealth taxes were repealed in most European countries that tried them (Sweden, Germany, Denmark, Austria, Netherlands, France) over capital flight and valuation problems.
  3. Broaden asset ownership: sovereign wealth funds (Norway, Alaska) — though state-held; baby bonds / universal basic capital as the non-state version.
  4. Capture-resistant government: sortition and citizens' assemblies (Ireland, France, Belgium), revolving-door cooling-off periods, lobbying registers, public campaign financing (with the free-speech trade-off).
  5. Polycentric governance (Ostrom): commons managed without privatization or central control.
  6. Federalism and exit — double-edged: lets citizens escape captured jurisdictions, lets capital play jurisdictions against each other (Hirschman).

## Part 2 — Reading List

Items marked **▶** are the recommended starting point in each section. "Short" means an article or a book under ~200 pages.

*Verification status (Sept 2026):* Albert (*Parecon*), Carson, Kinna, and Bookchin were checked against publisher and library records. Schweickart, Ellerman, Whyte & Whyte, Phillips & Rozworski, Cockshott & Cottrell, Nove, Kornai, Coase, and the classical anarchist texts were confirmed by sources consulted earlier. The remaining entries are canonical works listed from knowledge and not re-checked.

### Power, institutions, and the state/society problem

- **▶ Acemoglu & Robinson — *The Narrow Corridor* (2019).** The strong-state-vs-strong-society framing; built around exactly the two-sided fear of private and state concentration.
- **Acemoglu & Robinson — *Why Nations Fail* (2012).** Inclusive vs. extractive institutions; why some countries stay in the corridor.
- **Madison — Federalist Nos. 10 and 51 (1787–88).** Short. Factions, and the original "ambition must be made to counteract ambition."
- **Hirschman — *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970).** Short. When exit disciplines power and when it just lets it escape.
- **Buchanan & Tullock — *The Calculus of Consent* (1962).** Public choice: models officials as self-interested and asks which constitutional rules survive that assumption. The intellectual home of the government-tyranny worry.
- **▶ Ostrom — *Governing the Commons* (1990).** Nobel-winning fieldwork on communities managing shared resources without privatization or central control.
- **▶ James C. Scott — *Seeing Like a State* (1998).** Why top-down "legibility" schemes fail; written by an anarchist-sympathetic political scientist, so it bridges to the anarchist section. Belongs equally under the calculation debate — it is about planning failure.
- **Besley & Persson — *Pillars of Prosperity* (2011).** State capacity as a development achievement; why weak states are often more predatory.
- **Stigler — "The Theory of Economic Regulation" (1971).** Short article. The foundational regulatory-capture argument.
- **Hall & Soskice (eds.) — *Varieties of Capitalism* (2001).** The "coordinated vs. liberal market economies" framework; the academic basis for treating Germany and the Nordics as a distinct system.
- **Hayek — *The Road to Serfdom* (1944)** and **Friedman — *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962).** The canonical statements of the taxation-and-tyranny worry; the laissez-faire side of the argument.
- **Coase & Wang — *How China Became Capitalist* (2012).** The state-capitalism / developmental-state case, from the author of "The Nature of the Firm."

### Markets, planning, and the calculation debate

- **▶ Hayek — "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945).** ~15 pages. The whole knowledge-problem argument.
- **Mises — "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" (1920).** The original salvo.
- **Coase — "The Nature of the Firm" (1937).** Short. Islands of planning in a sea of markets.
- **Kornai — *The Socialist System* (1992).** The soft-budget-constraint account from inside actually-existing planning.
- **▶ Alec Nove — *The Economics of Feasible Socialism* (1983).** A socialist who took the calculation problem seriously and designed around it.
- **Schumpeter — *Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy* (1942).** Creative destruction, plus an even-handed narration of the debate.
- **Phillips & Rozworski — *The People's Republic of Walmart* (2019).** The modern pro-planning case built on corporate logistics.
- **Cockshott & Cottrell — *Towards a New Socialism* (1993).** The computational-planning proposal.

### Economic democracy, market socialism, worker ownership

- **▶ Schweickart — *After Capitalism* (2002).** The Economic Democracy model: worker self-management, markets for goods, socially controlled investment.
- **▶ Erik Olin Wright — *Envisioning Real Utopias* (2010).** The best survey of real-world experiments (Mondragón, participatory budgeting, Wikipedia, basic income) with honest assessment of each.
- **Robert Dahl — *A Preface to Economic Democracy* (1985).** Short. The political-theory case that democratic principles apply to firms.
- **Whyte & Whyte — *Making Mondragon* (1988).** The definitive institutional history; explains the capital-account mechanics.
- **David Ellerman — *The Democratic Worker-Owned Firm* (1990).** The "labor theory of property" argument against the employment contract itself.
- **Isabelle Ferreras — *Firms as Political Entities* (2017).** "Economic bicameralism": a workers' chamber alongside the shareholders' chamber. The most developed extension of codetermination.
- **John Roemer — *A Future for Socialism* (1994).** A different market-socialist design (coupon-based shares), for contrast with Schweickart.
- **Michael Albert — *Parecon: Life After Capitalism* (2003).** Participatory economics; the main non-market democratic proposal. (Albert is sole author; Hahnel co-wrote the earlier academic statements, *The Political Economy of Participatory Economics* and *Looking Forward*, both 1991.) Pair with Schweickart's 2006 critique, "Nonsense on Stilts: Michael Albert's Parecon" — a direct market-socialist vs. non-market exchange.
- **Blasi, Freeman & Kruse — *The Citizen's Share* (2013).** Empirical record of employee ownership in the U.S.
- **Richard Wolff — *Democracy at Work* (2012).** Accessible; worker self-directed enterprises.
- **Jäger & Noy — "What Does Codetermination Do?" (NBER Working Paper 28921, 2021).** The empirical basis for the codetermination claims in Part 1.
- **Pérotin — "What do we really know about worker co-operatives?" (Co-operatives UK, 2016).** The productivity meta-review cited in Part 1.
- **Hacker — "The Institutional Foundations of Middle-Class Democracy" (Policy Network, 2011).** Where "predistribution" was coined.

### Anarchist

- **Proudhon — *What Is Property?* (1840).** The mutualist foundation; possession vs. property.
- **Kropotkin — *The Conquest of Bread* (1892) and *Mutual Aid* (1902).** Anarcho-communism; *Mutual Aid* argues cooperation, not just competition, drives evolution and social order — it supports the anarchist premise rather than describing the system.
- **Bakunin — *Statism and Anarchy* (1873).** The collectivist critique of Marx, including the prediction that a "workers' state" becomes a new ruling class.
- **Rudolf Rocker — *Anarcho-Syndicalism: Theory and Practice* (1938).** The standard statement.
- **▶ Colin Ward — *Anarchy in Action* (1973).** Short, practical; treats anarchism as already present in how people organize. Good fit for an engineering mindset.
- **James C. Scott — *Two Cheers for Anarchism* (2012).** Short essays; the "two cheers" hedge is honest about limits.
- **▶ Kevin Carson — *Organization Theory: A Libertarian Perspective* (2008).** A mutualist applying the Coase firm-size question to argue that state intervention subsidizes firms beyond their efficient scale. Directly relevant to the concentration thread. *Note:* self-published (BookSurge, Center for a Stateless Society) by an independent scholar — a serious argument, not a peer-reviewed authority.
- **Sam Dolgoff (ed.) — *The Anarchist Collectives* (1974).** Primary documents from Spain 1936–39, the only large-scale test.
- **Murray Bookchin — *The Next Revolution* (2015).** Libertarian municipalism; the theoretical basis for Rojava's confederalism. *Note:* Bookchin broke with anarchism in 1999 and called his later politics "Communalism"; this posthumous collection is mostly post-break essays. Adjacent to anarchism rather than inside it.
- **Graeber & Wengrow — *The Dawn of Everything* (2021).** Argues the archaeological record shows far more variety in political organization than "state or chaos" allows. Anthropology by anarchists, not anarchist theory — background, not a statement of the tradition.
- **Ruth Kinna — *The Government of No One* (2019).** A modern scholarly map of the tradition before diving into primary sources.

### Concentration, capture, and the fixes

- **Piketty — *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014).** The r > g argument for why concentration is the default.
- **▶ Tim Wu — *The Curse of Bigness* (2018).** Short. The neo-Brandeisian case that concentration is a political problem, not just a price problem.
- **Lina Khan — "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" (2017, *Yale Law Journal*).** The article that relaunched the antitrust debate.
- **Henry George — *Progress and Poverty* (1879).** The land-value-tax argument; the core is in the first third.
- **Ackerman & Alstott — *The Stakeholder Society* (1999).** Universal basic capital.
- **Lessig — *Republic, Lost* (2011).** Money in politics as systemic rather than individual corruption.
- **Landemore — *Open Democracy* (2020).** The strongest case for sortition and citizens' assemblies as capture-resistant institutions.

### A compressed path

Hayek (1945) → Nove → Ostrom → *Envisioning Real Utopias* → *The Narrow Corridor* → Colin Ward → Carson. This retraces the conversation with the strongest version of each side.
